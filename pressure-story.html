Key words, in plain language first
Pressure
The steady push a gas makes on each bit of a surface, from the hits of its molecules. More hits, or harder hits, means more pressure.
Kilopascal, kPa
A unit of pressure, named for Blaise Pascal, who showed in the 1640s that air pressure drops as you climb a mountain. Air at sea level pushes at about 101 kPa, which is also called 1 atmosphere (1 atm).
Volume
The room a gas has, measured here in liters (L).
Boyle’s law
For the same gas at the same temperature, pressure × volume stays the same. Squeeze it into half the room and the pressure doubles. Named for Robert Boyle, who measured it in 1662.
Kelvin, K
A temperature scale that starts at the coldest possible temperature. Add 273 to degrees Celsius. Gas math uses kelvin because the push of a gas is proportional to it.
Ideal gas law
P × V = n × R × T. The area of the rectangle (P × V) is set by how many molecules there are (n) and how hot they are (T). R is a fixed number that makes the units agree.
About this model

The gas. An ideal gas: P = nRT ÷ V. The bag starts in Denver holding 0.50 L of nitrogen at 20 °C, and its wall is treated as slack, so it moves freely until the push from inside matches the push from outside.

The air outside. Pressure at each elevation comes from the standard atmosphere: 101.3 kPa at sea level, 83.4 kPa at 5,280 ft (Denver) and 66.6 kPa at 11,158 ft (Eisenhower Tunnel). Real weather moves these by a few percent from day to day.

The box. 36 molecules stand for the roughly 10²² in the bag. They are drawn to shape (N≡N 1.10 Å, O=O 1.21 Å), with about one oxygen for every four nitrogens in the outside air. The hit count is a real count of what you see. The pressure numbers come from the gas law.

Simplified. Time is slowed enormously. Molecules are drawn larger, compared with the space between them, than they really are. The car is treated as staying at 20 °C. A real bag stops stretching once it is tight, and then the pressure inside stays a little higher than outside. “The wall is held still” in step 5 is a pause for the eye. A real bag wall moves as the air thins.

For teachers: what this is

This is a pilot of a second “story version” page, built the same way as the equilibrium story. It sits beside the current gas particles and gas laws tools and changes nothing there. It supports your lesson and does not replace it.

  • One molecule first, then the crowd. Students see one hit as one push, then a crowd of hits as a steady push, before the word pressure appears.
  • Both sides of the wall. The common mistake is that the gas inside “expands.” Here students see the push from outside drop first, a gap open, and the wall move until the pushes match.
  • Boyle’s law as a picture. Pressure is the height of a rectangle and volume is its width. Students drag the wall and the area holds. Its corner traces the P–V curve.
  • PV = nRT as a picture. Molecules and temperature set the area.
  • Students commit first, then use it. Three guesses, one calculation with a typed answer, the causal chain put in order, and Copy for Canvas.

Nothing here needs a login, and nothing a student types leaves the browser. Page visits are counted without names.
