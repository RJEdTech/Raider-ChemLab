Key words, in plain language first
Ion
An atom, or a team of atoms, with a charge, because it lost or gained electrons. A positive ion is a cation. A negative ion is an anion.
Ionic compound
Positive and negative ions held together because opposite charges attract. It usually has a metal in it. The charges must cancel, and that sets the formula.
Molecular compound
Nonmetal atoms that share electrons. Nothing forces one recipe, so the name counts the atoms with prefixes.
Polyatomic ion
“Poly” means many. A team of atoms that carries one charge and stays together, such as nitrate, NO₃⁻.
Roman numeral in a name
The charge of a metal that has more than one possible charge. Iron(III) means Fe³⁺.
Prefixes
mono 1, di 2, tri 3, tetra 4, penta 5. They come from Greek number words.
The -ide ending
The second element in a two-element compound changes its ending: chlorine becomes chloride, oxygen becomes oxide.
About these pictures

Ions are drawn to scale with each other, from their measured ionic radii: Na⁺ 102 pm, Mg²⁺ 72, Al³⁺ 54, Ca²⁺ 100, Fe²⁺ 78, Fe³⁺ 65, Cl⁻ 181, O²⁻ 140. The colors only tell the ions apart.

Simplified. A real ionic solid is a huge repeating crystal, so the picture of a few separate ions shows the ratio, not a molecule. The pieces in a team ion are drawn at the same scale with their real bond lengths. The shapes of N₂O, NO, NO₂ and N₂O₄ are real. For N₂O₃ and N₂O₅ the bonds are right and the angles are approximate. Sulfate is a tetrahedron, drawn here looking straight down one of its axes.

Left out on purpose. Acids, hydrates, older names like ferric and ferrous, and metals other than iron that have a choice of charge. The naming trainer covers more.

For teachers: what this is

This is a pilot of a “story version” page for naming, built like the equilibrium story and the gas pressure story. It sits beside the current naming trainer and changes nothing there. It supports your lesson and does not replace it.

  • One idea ties the rules together: a name only has to say what you could not work out. Charges force an ionic recipe, so no prefixes. A metal with a choice needs its charge stated. Shared electrons force nothing, so the name counts.
  • Students build before they name. They add ions until a plus bar and a minus bar are the same length, and the formula and name appear from what they built.
  • The usual mix-up is the target: prefixes on ionic compounds, and a missing Roman numeral. Each wrong name gets a hint that says why.
  • Turn it in. Four names from tiles, an explanation in their own words, and Copy for Canvas.

Nothing here needs a login, and nothing a student types leaves the browser. Page visits are counted without names.
