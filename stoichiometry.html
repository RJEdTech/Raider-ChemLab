    Part 1

    What is stoichiometry?

    Definition

    Stoichiometry answers one question: if you start with this much of one chemical, how much of another do you get?

    Example: burn 4.00 g of hydrogen. How much water do you get? The equation tells you which particles go together: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.

    A balanced equation is a recipe that counts. A s’more takes 2 graham crackers and 1 marshmallow. That recipe tells you that 10 crackers need 5 marshmallows, no matter how much each one weighs.

    2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O: 2 mol H₂ react with 1 mol O₂ to make 2 mol H₂O
    Quick reminder from the last unit

    Molecules are far too small and too many to count one by one, so chemists count them by weighing, the way a bank counts pennies. They count in groups called moles: 6.022 × 10²³ molecules in each, like a very big dozen. Molar mass is how much one mole weighs, in grams. You add it up from the periodic table: H₂O is 2(1.008) + 16.00 = 18.02 g per mole.

    One mole of…How many moleculesWeighs
    hydrogen, H₂6.022 × 10²³2.02 g
    oxygen, O₂6.022 × 10²³32.00 g
    water, H₂O6.022 × 10²³18.02 g

    Same number of molecules every time, different weight, like a dozen eggs and a dozen bowling balls.

    Reminder from the reactions unit

    Burning hydrogen makes water

    “Burning” means joining with oxygen from the air. The atoms swap partners: two hydrogen molecules and one oxygen molecule become two water molecules. No atoms are lost. They just rearrange.

    Step 1 of 3: two H₂ molecules and one O₂ molecule
    Atoms: 4 H, 2 O (never changes)Molecules: 3
    Before
    H₂ 4.04 gO₂ 32.00 g
    After
    H₂O 36.04 g

    Weights for 2 mol H₂ and 1 mol O₂. Watch what happens to them.

    Why care? This is why one gallon of gasoline, about 6 pounds, turns into about 20 pounds of CO₂: oxygen from the air joins it, just like the oxygen joins the hydrogen here. You’ll work it out yourself in Part 8.

    The blue and green hydrogens each end up inside one water. Count the atoms: 4 H and 2 O before, 4 H and 2 O after. That is why a hydrogen-powered car’s tailpipe drips water, and why the space shuttle’s main engines left a cloud of water vapor.

    The equation says the same thing in symbols: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. The big numbers in front count the molecules in the picture.

    Read an equation as a recipe

    2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

    2 mol of hydrogen react with 1 mol of oxygen to make 2 mol of water.

    Double it and you get 4 : 2 : 4. The ratio stays the same.

    Why moles, not grams?

    A dozen eggs and a dozen bowling balls are both 12, but they weigh very different amounts. The equation counts like that: 2 H₂ for every 1 O₂, not 2 grams for every 1 gram. So we change grams into a count (moles) first.

    EquationThe ratioRead it as
    2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O2 mol H₂O / 1 mol O₂2 water for every 1 oxygen
    N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃2 mol NH₃ / 1 mol N₂2 ammonia for every 1 nitrogen
    C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O3 mol CO₂ / 1 mol C₃H₈3 carbon dioxide for every 1 propane
    2Al + 3CuCl₂ → 2AlCl₃ + 3Cu3 mol Cu / 2 mol Al3 copper for every 2 aluminum

    Part 2

    The road map

    Every problem takes the same three moves. Here they are for one problem: burn 4.00 g of hydrogen. How much water do you get?

    2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
    Step 1Grams to moles4.00 g H₂ ÷ 2.02 = 1.98 mol H₂

    Count by weighing. A bank counts pennies by weighing them: 250 g ÷ 2.5 g each = 100 pennies. A mole of H₂ weighs 2.02 g, so 4.00 g ÷ 2.02 g = 1.98 moles.

    Step 2Moles to moles1.98 mol H₂ × (2 H₂O / 2 H₂) = 1.98 mol H₂O

    Same count, more weight. Each H₂ ends up inside one H₂O, so the number of molecules (moles) stays the same. But each water is heavier: it picked up an O. The arrow means “makes,” not “equals.” What you want goes on top.

    Step 3Moles to grams1.98 mol H₂O × 18.02 = 35.7 g H₂O

    Turn the count back into a weight. Like 100 pennies × 2.5 g = 250 g: 1.98 moles × 18.02 g per mole = 35.7 g. Much more than the 4.00 g of hydrogen: the extra is oxygen that joined.

    Why don’t we use the O₂?

    Oxygen is part of the reaction: every water molecule needs one O. But the question is only about hydrogen and water, and the air has plenty of oxygen. So the hydrogen decides how much water forms, and the ratio links just those two: what you have and what you want.

    A picture with a few molecules: 4 H₂ use only 2 of the 6 O₂. The hydrogen runs out first.

    When there isn’t enough of one ingredient, it runs out first and decides the answer. That is the limiting reactant, in Part 5.

    The same problem in one line

    4.00 g H₂ × 1 mol H₂2.02 g H₂ × 2 mol H₂O2 mol H₂ × 18.02 g H₂O1 mol H₂O = 35.7 g H₂O

    Each fraction cancels the unit before it: grams of H₂, then moles of H₂, then moles of H₂O. The count stays 1.98 moles in the middle; the weight goes from 4.00 g to 35.7 g because oxygen joined.

    Part 5

    Which runs out first?

    The idea

    When you mix two reactants, one usually runs out first. It is the limiting reactant, and it decides how much product you get. The other one is in excess: some is left over.

    A s’more takes 2 crackers and 1 marshmallow. With 10 crackers and 4 marshmallows, you make only 4 s’mores. The marshmallows are limiting.

    Four steps

    1. Change both reactants to moles.

    2. Use each mole ratio to find how much product each one could make.

    3. The reactant that makes less product is limiting.

    4. Change that smaller amount of product to grams.

      Part 8 · after you submit
      Why it matters
      
  
      Where people use this math, and what it tells you about your own world.
      
  
      
    
      How this changes the world
      
    
      
      
      🌾
      Feeding the world
      Fritz Haber (Nobel Prize, 1918) and Carl Bosch (Nobel Prize, 1931) learned to make ammonia by the ton: N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃. Fertilizer made from it grows food for about half the people on Earth. Factories use the mole ratio to feed in exactly 3 H₂ for every N₂.
      
      
      🚗
      Airbags save lives
      NHTSA estimates frontal airbags had saved 70,059 lives in the U.S. through 2019. Every one of them depends on engineers getting the powder-to-gas math right.
      
      
      🌍
      Measuring climate change
      The EPA’s number, 8,887 g of CO₂ per gallon of gasoline, comes from this math. You can’t cut pollution you can’t measure.
      
      
      💧
      Clean energy
      Hydrogen fuel cells make electricity from today’s reaction, 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. Their only exhaust is water.
      
    
      
  
      
  
      
    
      A puzzle from the EPA
      
    
      How can 6 pounds of gas make 20 pounds of CO₂?
      
    
      A gallon of gasoline weighs about 6 pounds. The EPA says burning it makes about 8,887 grams (about 20 pounds) of CO₂. Stoichiometry explains it: each CO₂ is mostly oxygen, and that oxygen comes from the air, not the tank.
      
    
      2C₈H₁₈ + 25O₂ → 16CO₂ + 18H₂O
      
    
      You’ll work it out yourself in the first real problem below.
      
  
      
  
      
    
      How people use it
      
    
      
      
      🚗
      Airbags
      Engineers pack exactly enough propellant to fill the bag in a crash. Too little and it won’t protect you; too much and it can burst.
      g N₂ → mol N₂ → mol NaN₃ → g NaN₃
      
      
      💊
      Making medicine
      A drug factory works out how much of each starting chemical to buy. Extra of the cheap one makes sure none of the expensive one is wasted.
      limiting reactant = the expensive one
      
      
      🌾
      Fertilizer
      Most farm fertilizer starts as ammonia: N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃. Plants grown with it feed about half the people on Earth.
      1 mol N₂ → 2 mol NH₃
      
      
      🚀
      Rockets
      Rockets carry their own oxygen. Engineers load fuel and oxygen by the mole ratio, because every extra kilogram has to be lifted.
      2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

      Use it in your life
      
    
        
      
      • Do: When a recipe or a reaction says “2 to 1,” ask whether it means by count or by weight. Why: equations count particles (moles), not grams.
        • 
      
      • Do: Before you shop for a party, find the ingredient that runs out first. Why: that limiting ingredient decides how many you can make; extra of everything else is wasted.
        • 
      
      • Do: In lab, measure the limiting reactant most carefully. Why: it sets your answer. The excess one only has to be “enough.”
        • 
      
      • Do: Remember 1 gallon of gasoline ≈ 20 pounds of CO₂. Why: it’s a quick way to estimate a car trip’s exhaust, and now you know where that mass comes from.
