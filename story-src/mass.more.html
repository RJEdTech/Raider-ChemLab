Key words, in plain language first
Isotopes
Atoms of the same element with different masses. They have the same number of protons and different numbers of neutrons. The word means “same place”: they share one square on the periodic table.
Mass number
Protons plus neutrons in one atom. Always a whole number. Neon-20 has mass number 20.
Abundance
How common an isotope is: its share of all the atoms of that element, as a percent.
Average atomic mass
The balance point of all the atoms of an element: each isotope’s mass times its share, added up. It is the decimal number on the periodic table.
Atomic mass unit, u
The unit for the mass of one atom. One proton or one neutron is about 1 u.
Mass spectrometer
A machine that sorts charged atoms by mass, using a magnet to turn them.

The machine. Each atom you fire is chosen by chance with those odds, so your counts will differ a little from a classmate’s, and a bigger crowd lands closer to the true shares. The landing spot is drawn in proportion to the mass, as in a real instrument where all the ions enter at one speed.

Simplified. Thomson’s 1913 apparatus spread the atoms into curved lines on a photographic plate. The half-circle design shown here came a few years later. Nuclei show the right number of protons and neutrons, drawn flat. Neon-21 is real but rare: about 1 atom in 370.

For teachers: what this is

This is a pilot of a “story version” page for average atomic mass, built like the other three ChemLab stories. It sits beside the current average atomic mass tool and changes nothing there. It supports your lesson and does not replace it.

  • Students weigh the atoms first. They fire neon through a mass spectrometer and see two piles before the word isotope appears.
  • The weighted average is a balance point. The crowd stands on a beam, and the student drags the pivot until it is level at 20.18.
  • Both directions of the classic problem: shares to average (chlorine, typed answer, with a specific hint for 36), and average to shares (boron).
  • The usual mix-up is the target: mass number (one atom, whole) against average atomic mass (the crowd, decimal).

The story is real: J. J. Thomson found two kinds of neon in 1913, and Francis Aston won the 1922 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the mass spectrograph and the isotopes it revealed.
