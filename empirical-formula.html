    Part 1

    What is an empirical formula?

    Why learn this?

    How do we know water is H₂O? No one has ever looked at a water molecule and counted its atoms. Chemists measured that water is 11.2% hydrogen and 88.8% oxygen by mass, then used the method in this lesson to turn those percents into a formula. By the end, you can check it yourself.

    Definition

    An empirical formula shows the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound.

    A molecular formula shows the actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule. The molecular formula is always a whole-number multiple (n) of the empirical formula.

    Think of a recipe. Pancakes take 1 cup of mix, 2 eggs and 1 cup of milk. A double batch is 2 : 4 : 2 and a triple batch is 3 : 6 : 3, but the ratio is always 1 : 2 : 1.

    The empirical formula is the recipe: the simplest ratio. The molecular formula is how big the batch really is. n is the number of batches.

    CH₂O × 6 = C₆H₁₂O₆ (glucose is 6 batches)

    Reduce it like a fraction

    Glucose has 6 C, 12 H and 6 O. Every subscript is divisible by 6, so divide them all by 6.

    C6H12O6 ÷ 6 → CH2O

    The ratio 6 : 12 : 6 is the same as 1 : 2 : 1. Only the simplest version is the empirical formula.

    Why chemists need it

    When a lab analyzes an unknown compound (for example by combustion analysis), it measures mass percent of each element. Mass data can only tell you the ratio of atoms. That ratio is the empirical formula. To get the real molecule, you also need the molar mass.

    Part 2

    Percent composition → empirical formula

    Percent composition tells you what percent of a compound's mass comes from each element. Four moves turn those percents into a formula. Learn them as a rhyme.

    Step 1Percent to grams% → g

    Assume a 100 g sample. Then 40.0% carbon is simply 40.0 g of carbon.

    Step 2Grams to molesg × (1 mol / M g) → mol

    Atoms combine by count, not by mass. Moles count particles, so convert each mass using the element's molar mass.

    Step 3Divide by smallestmol ÷ mol → ratio

    Divide every mole value by the smallest one. The smallest becomes 1, and the units cancel, leaving a pure ratio.

    Step 4Make it wholeratio × n → whole numbers

    Subscripts must be whole numbers. If a ratio is not within about 0.1 of a whole number, multiply all ratios by the same number.

    "Make it whole" multipliers

    Never round 1.5 up to 2. A decimal like that is a signal to multiply.

    Part 5

    From empirical to molecular formula

    The idea

    The empirical formula is the smallest repeating "unit." A real molecule contains n of those units. The molar mass tells you how many.

    n = molar mass g/molempirical formula mass g/mol

    The g/mol units cancel, so n is a pure whole number.

    Three steps

    1. Add the atomic masses in the empirical formula to get the empirical formula mass (g/mol).

    2. Divide the given molar mass by it to find n.

    3. Multiply every subscript in the empirical formula by n.

      Why it matters
      
  
      Why this is worth learning, and where people use it.
      
  
      
    
      Now check it yourself
      
    
      How do we know water is H₂O?
      
    
      Part 1 asked this. Chemists in the 1800s did what you just did: water is 11.2% hydrogen and 88.8% oxygen by mass, so:
      
    
      11.2 g H ÷ 1.008 = 11.1 mol  ·  88.8 g O ÷ 16.00 = 5.55 mol  →  2 : 1  →  H₂O
      
    
      John Dalton, who gave us the modern idea of atoms, first guessed water was HO. Better measurements and this math showed it was H₂O. Every formula in your textbook was first found this way.
      
  
      
  
      
    
      You’ll use it all year
      Grams → moles is the move behind every reaction problem in the next unit: how much product a reaction makes, or how much of one chemical you need for another.
      
    
      It still answers “what is this?”
      When a lab makes a new medicine or finds an unknown powder, the first test reports the percent of each element. The same four moves turn it into a formula.
      
  
      
  
      
    
      How people use it
      
    
      Real people do this math every day
      
    
      
      
      🌱
      Fertilizer bags
      The first number on a bag is percent nitrogen. Urea is sold as 46-0-0. A gardener uses it to know how much to spread.
      10.0 kg × 0.467 = 4.67 kg N
      
      
      💊
      New medicines
      When chemists make a new drug, an analyzer burns a few milligrams and reports % C, H and N. Journals want those numbers within 0.4% of the formula. Fritz Pregl won the 1923 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for making this work on tiny samples.
      % → mol → ratio → formula
      
      
      ⛏️
      Mining iron
      Magnetite (Fe₃O₄) is 72.4% iron and hematite (Fe₂O₃) is 69.9%. Percent iron helps decide which ore is worth digging up and shipping.
      1,000 kg × 0.724 = 724 kg Fe
      
      
      🔎
      Forensics
      An unknown powder gets two tests. One gives the element ratio. A mass spectrometer gives the molar mass. Together they give the molecular formula: the same two steps you just did. Raider ForensicsLab
      n = molar mass ÷ EFM
      
      
      🧪
      Your MgO lab
      Burning magnesium and weighing the ash is an empirical formula experiment. The extra mass is oxygen from the air. Try it in Raider ChemLab
      mol O ÷ mol Mg = 1.00 → MgO

      Use it in your life
      
    
        
      
      • Do: Read the three numbers on a fertilizer bag before you buy one. Why: 46-0-0 urea has more nitrogen per kilogram than 34-0-0, so you spread less of it. Less extra fertilizer washes into streams.
        • 
      
      • Do: On a supplement label, look for the amount of the element, not of the compound. Why: calcium carbonate is only 40.0% calcium, so a 1,250 mg tablet gives 500 mg of calcium.
        • 
      
      • Do: To turn salt into sodium, multiply grams of salt by 0.393. Why: nutrition labels list sodium, and table salt is 39.3% sodium. The 2,300 mg Daily Value is about one teaspoon of salt.
        • 
      
      • Do: In lab, write every mass with its unit and heat to constant mass. Why: the formula comes from mass ratios. One misread mass can change MgO into a formula that doesn’t exist.
