Key words, in plain language first
Reversible reaction
A reaction that runs both ways. N₂O₄ splits into two NO₂, and two NO₂ join back into N₂O₄. The double arrow ⇌ shows both directions.
Equilibrium
From the Latin for “equal balance.” Splits and joins happen equally often, so the amounts stop changing.
Dynamic equilibrium
“Dynamic” means still moving. The amounts hold steady, but single molecules keep splitting and joining.
Shifts right, shifts left
A new balance with more of what is on the right of the arrow (here, NO₂) is a shift right. More N₂O₄ is a shift left.
Le Châtelier’s principle
Disturb a balanced reaction and it shifts to partly undo the change. Named for Henry Le Châtelier, who described the pattern in 1884. The cause is the two rates, as you saw.
Endothermic and exothermic
Splitting N₂O₄ takes energy in (endo, “into”). Joining two NO₂ gives energy off (exo, “out of”). That is why heat helps splitting.
Concentration, written [ ]
How crowded a substance is: moles in each liter. [NO₂] is read “the concentration of NO₂.”
Equilibrium constant, K
The number you get from [NO₂]² ÷ [N₂O₄] once the mixture is balanced. Every mixture at the same temperature ends at the same K. The K comes from the German word Konstante.
About this model

Data. N₂O₄(g) ⇌ 2 NO₂(g), Kc = 4.6 × 10⁻³ at 25 °C and ΔH° = +57.2 kJ/mol. These are the same textbook values the current ChemLab equilibrium tool uses, so the numbers agree: 0.050 mol/L of N₂O₄ settles at 0.0430 M N₂O₄ and 0.0141 M NO₂ at 25 °C. K at other temperatures comes from the van ’t Hoff equation.

Rates. Splits each second = ksplit × N₂O₄. Joins each second = kjoin × NO₂² ÷ room. Joining is given no energy barrier, so kjoin stays the same at every temperature and ksplit = kjoin × K carries all of the temperature change. That is close to the real reaction, where the energy needed to split is about the same as ΔH.

The box. It shows about 60 to 90 molecules, drawn to their real shapes (N–N 1.78 Å, N–O about 1.2 Å). The amounts follow the rate laws, and each split or join in the box is one of those events. A split is shown at a collision. A join is shown when two NO₂ touch.

Simplified. The real reaction settles in a tiny fraction of a second, so time here is slowed down enormously. The gas is treated as ideal. Splitting is treated as depending only on the amount of N₂O₄, which holds at ordinary pressures. Molecules are drawn larger, compared with the space between them, than they really are, so you can see their shapes. The brown glow around each NO₂ is a marker so you can find them. The tube color follows Beer’s law, with its strength set to match the classroom demonstration.

For teachers: what this is

This is a pilot. I built it to try one idea: teach equilibrium as a single story in which the picture carries the cause, and the words only caption it. It sits beside the current equilibrium tool and changes nothing there. It supports your lesson and does not replace it. Use it before, during or after you teach the topic.

  • One molecule first, then the crowd. Students snap one bond with a hard hit, then watch sixty molecules do the same thing by chance.
  • Students commit first. They guess the color change, draw the NO₂ curve before it runs, and predict which rate falls when the gas spreads out.
  • Le Châtelier with a cause. Heat and extra room are shown through the two rates: one jumps or drops, a gap opens, and the other catches up.
  • K as a picture. Joins are a square (NO₂ × NO₂). Splits are a strip (N₂O₄ long, K high). Balance is where the areas match, so [NO₂]² = K × [N₂O₄].
  • Turn it in. The last step has students order the causal chain, explain it in their own words, and copy their work for Canvas.

The story is real. On August 2, 2020, the Crew Dragon Endeavour splashed down with Bob Behnken and Doug Hurley. Once the capsule was on the recovery ship, opening the hatch was delayed about half an hour while the team purged leftover dinitrogen tetroxide vapor (rocket crews call it nitrogen tetroxide, or NTO). Sources: NASA Commercial Crew blog, “Astronauts Doing Well Inside Crew Dragon Spacecraft,” Aug. 2, 2020; SpaceNews, Aug. 2, 2020; NASA, “45 Years Ago: Apollo Returns Home to End ASTP,” for the 1975 exposure.
