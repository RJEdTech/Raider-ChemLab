Part 1

The story

You’re camping in the mountains in winter, near an old mine. Overnight a bear knocks over your table, spills your fresh water and eats all your food.

The creek next to camp is frozen. You chip a bucket of ice out of it. It’s cloudy with dirt and grit from the mine, and a test kit shows a chemical from the mine that would make you sick. You have about a day before help arrives.

The sun is strong in the afternoon, the snow is cold, and you have a camp stove with 6 cans of fuel.

Part 4

The real numbers

ParticleReal size (about)What catches it
Water molecule0.0000003 mmNothing. It goes through every filter here.
Dissolved mine metalsabout the same as waterNo filter here. Distilling leaves them behind, and lime can make them settle out.
Bacteria0.001–0.002 mmToo small for a coffee filter. Boiling kills them.
Fine dirt (silt)0.004–0.06 mmA coffee filter catches most of it.
Sand and grit0.06–2 mmA kitchen strainer catches the bigger pieces.

Why the chemical is hard: it’s dissolved, mixed in molecule by molecule, so it’s about as small as water itself. No hole is small enough to let water through and stop the chemical. Instead you use a difference in boiling: water turns to steam and the chemical stays in the pot.

Mountain boiling: water boils at 100 °C at sea level but only about 95 °C in Denver and about 90 °C at 10,000 feet, because there’s less air pressing down. That’s why the CDC says to boil water for 1 minute, but 3 minutes above 6,500 feet. It also says boiling does not remove chemicals.

Part 5

Why care?

This happened in Colorado. On August 5, 2015, about 3 million gallons of mine water poured out of the Gold King Mine near Silverton and turned the Animas River orange. Towns downstream had to stop using the river for their water.
